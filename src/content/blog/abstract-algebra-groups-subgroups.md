A group is the first algebraic structure in which an operation can be performed without leaving the set, has a neutral element, and can always be undone. That simple package is enough to formalize symmetry, modular multiplication, elliptic-curve addition, permutation composition, and many other constructions used later in cryptography.

## 1. Definition

A **group** is a pair $(G,\star)$ consisting of a set $G$ and a binary operation $\star:G\times G\to G$ such that, for all $a,b,c\in G$:

1. **Closure:** $a\star b\in G$.
2. **Associativity:** $(a\star b)\star c=a\star(b\star c)$.
3. **Identity:** there exists $e\in G$ such that
   $$e\star a=a\star e=a.$$
4. **Inverse:** for every $a\in G$ there exists $a^{-1}\in G$ with
   $$a\star a^{-1}=a^{-1}\star a=e.$$

The identity equation is worth writing carefully: it returns **the element $a$**, not the identity. A surprisingly common transcription error is to write $ae=ea=e$, which would collapse the structure.

If $a\star b=b\star a$ for every $a,b$, then $G$ is **abelian**.

## 2. First consequences

The axioms imply several facts rather than requiring them as extra assumptions:

- the identity is unique;
- each inverse is unique;
- $(a^{-1})^{-1}=a$;
- $(ab)^{-1}=b^{-1}a^{-1}$;
- cancellation holds: $ax=ay\Rightarrow x=y$ and $xa=ya\Rightarrow x=y$.

These are excellent exercises because each uses only associativity, the identity, and inverses.

## 3. Core examples

### Integers under addition

$(\mathbb Z,+)$ is an infinite abelian group. The identity is $0$ and the inverse of $a$ is $-a$.

### Nonzero residues modulo a prime

For a prime $p$,
$$
\mathbb F_p^\times=\{1,2,\dots,p-1\}
$$
is an abelian group under multiplication modulo $p$. Every nonzero residue has an inverse.

For composite $n$, the correct multiplicative group is not all nonzero residues but the **units**
$$
(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^\times=\{a\bmod n:\gcd(a,n)=1\}.
$$

### Permutations

The permutations of $n$ objects form the symmetric group $S_n$ under composition. For $n\ge 3$, this group is nonabelian, so it is a useful reminder that order can matter.

## 4. Subgroups

A subset $H\subseteq G$ is a **subgroup**, written $H\le G$, if it is itself a group under the operation inherited from $G$.

A convenient subgroup test is:

> A nonempty subset $H\subseteq G$ is a subgroup if $xy^{-1}\in H$ for all $x,y\in H$.

This packages closure and inverses into a single criterion.

## 5. Generated and cyclic groups

For $g\in G$, the subgroup generated by $g$ is
$$
\langle g\rangle=\{g^k:k\in\mathbb Z\}
$$
in multiplicative notation, or $\{kg:k\in\mathbb Z\}$ in additive notation.

A group is **cyclic** if $G=\langle g\rangle$ for some $g$. Such a $g$ is a generator.

Finite cyclic groups are particularly important because their subgroup structure is completely controlled by divisors of $|G|$. If $G=\langle g\rangle$ has order $n$, then
$$
\operatorname{ord}(g^k)=\frac{n}{\gcd(n,k)}.
$$

## 6. Element order

The **order** of $g\in G$ is the least positive integer $m$ such that $g^m=e$, if such an $m$ exists. Otherwise $g$ has infinite order.

Lagrange's theorem gives the fundamental finite-group constraint:
$$
H\le G\quad\Longrightarrow\quad |H|\mid |G|.
$$
In particular,
$$
\operatorname{ord}(g)\mid |G|.
$$

This is the structural reason that subgroup order matters in discrete-logarithm systems.

## 7. Where this leads

The next step is not to collect more examples, but to study **maps that preserve the operation**. Homomorphisms expose kernels, images, cosets, normal subgroups, and quotient groups—the language used to compare groups rather than only manipulate elements inside one group.
