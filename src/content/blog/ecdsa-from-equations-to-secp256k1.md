ECDSA keeps the DSA-style nonce equation but moves the group operation from finite-field exponentiation to scalar multiplication on an elliptic curve.

Understanding it is easier if we keep two moduli separate from the beginning:

- $p$ defines the coordinate field $\mathbb{F}_p$;
- $n$ is the order of the subgroup generated by the base point $G$.

Coordinate arithmetic is modulo $p$. Signature scalars are modulo $n$.

## Key generation

Choose a private scalar

$$
d\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
$$

and compute the public point

$$
Q=dG.
$$

Recovering $d$ from $Q$ is the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem.

## Signing

Let $z$ be the appropriately mapped hash of the message. Choose a fresh nonce

$$
k\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}.
$$

Compute

$$
R=kG=(x_R,y_R),
$$

$$
r=x_R\bmod n,
$$

and

$$
s=k^{-1}(z+rd)\bmod n.
$$

If $r=0$ or $s=0$, choose another nonce.

The signature is $(r,s)$.

## Verification

The verifier first checks that $r$ and $s$ lie in the valid scalar range. Then compute

$$
w=s^{-1}\bmod n,
$$

$$
u_1=zw\bmod n,
\qquad
u_2=rw\bmod n,
$$

and

$$
X=u_1G+u_2Q.
$$

The signature is valid when $X$ is not the point at infinity and

$$
x_X\bmod n=r.
$$

## Why verification works

Since $Q=dG$,

$$
X=(u_1+u_2d)G.
$$

Substituting the definitions of $u_1$ and $u_2$ gives

$$
X=w(z+rd)G.
$$

From the signing equation,

$$
s=k^{-1}(z+rd),
$$

so

$$
w(z+rd)=s^{-1}(z+rd)=k \pmod n.
$$

Therefore

$$
X=kG=R,
$$

which reproduces the $x$-coordinate from which $r$ was derived.

## secp256k1

The recovered notes contained a from-scratch secp256k1 implementation. secp256k1 uses the curve

$$
y^2=x^3+7\pmod p
$$

with

$$
p=2^{256}-2^{32}-977.
$$

It is widely known from Bitcoin and other systems. It is useful for learning because its coefficient $a=0$ makes the short-Weierstrass equation compact.

It is important, however, not to infer that every ECDSA standard profile uses secp256k1. NIST SP 800-186 lists NIST-approved curve families for U.S. Government applications; secp256k1 is used in other ecosystems but is not one of the NIST P-curves listed for ECDSA use.

## Point arithmetic edge cases

A teaching implementation must correctly represent the point at infinity and handle:

- adding the identity,
- adding a point to its inverse,
- point doubling when the tangent denominator vanishes,
- scalar zero,
- public-point validation.

Many minimal scripts omit one of these because their toy inputs never trigger it. That makes them useful derivations, not reusable libraries.

## Hash-to-integer handling

ECDSA signs an integer derived from the message digest. The mapping is specified by the signature profile; one should not casually reduce or truncate in an ad-hoc way.

The older Part1 examples used SHA-1 in several places because they were educational/historical. The cleaned article keeps the equations but does not present SHA-1 as a modern default.

## Nonce generation

Random nonce generation must be uniform and secret. RFC 6979 provides a deterministic ECDSA nonce derivation that is compatible with ordinary ECDSA verification.
