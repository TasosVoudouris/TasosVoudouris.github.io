This is the first article in the series where we can finally say:

> **We are building a cryptographic protocol.**

Not a helper function. Not only a number-theoretic identity. A protocol involving two parties who communicate over a public channel.

Alice and Bob will exchange public information and end up computing the same secret group element without ever transmitting that final value directly.

That remains one of the most elegant ideas in cryptography.

And because the purpose of **Cryptography From Zero** is not to treat cryptography as a black box, we will do three things:

```text
BUILD the protocol
        ↓
prove why it works
        ↓
BREAK the tiny version
```

The break is just as important as the construction.

A protocol can be mathematically correct while being cryptographically useless if the supposedly hard problem is easy for the chosen parameters.

![Toy Diffie-Hellman exchange and brute-force attack](/images/blog/05-diffie-hellman-build-break.svg)

*Alice and Bob derive the same group element. Eve sees the public values. With tiny parameters, she can simply search the exponent space.*

## Building Diffie-Hellman

From the previous articles, we already have the algebraic language we need.

Let

$$
G=\langle g\rangle
$$

be a cyclic group generated by $g$, with group order $q$.

Alice chooses a secret exponent

$$
a\in\mathbb Z_q
$$

and computes the public group element

$$
A=g^a.
$$

She sends $A$ to Bob.

Bob independently chooses

$$
b\in\mathbb Z_q
$$

and computes

$$
B=g^b.
$$

He sends $B$ to Alice.

Everything crossing the network can be public:

```text
group description

generator g

A = g^a

B = g^b
```

Only the exponents

$$
a,\qquad b
$$

must remain secret.

Alice receives $B$ and computes

$$
Z_A=B^a.
$$

Bob receives $A$ and computes

$$
Z_B=A^b.
$$

Why should they obtain the same value?

Substitute the definitions.

For Alice,

$$
Z_A
=
B^a
=
(g^b)^a
=
g^{ba}.
$$

For Bob,

$$
Z_B
=
A^b
=
(g^a)^b
=
g^{ab}.
$$

Since multiplication of the integer exponents is commutative,

$$
ab=ba,
$$

we obtain

$$
\boxed{
Z_A=Z_B=g^{ab}
}
$$

and both participants derive the same group element.

That is the entire correctness argument.

The equality does not depend on luck or on some special choice of numbers. It follows directly from exponentiation in the group.

This is a useful point to separate **correctness** from **security**.

> **Correctness:** Why do Alice and Bob obtain the same value?

The group algebra answers that question.

But there is another question:

> **Security:** Why should an observer who sees $g$, $g^a$, and $g^b$ be unable to compute $g^{ab}$?

That question is not answered merely by proving

$$
(g^a)^b=(g^b)^a.
$$

Security requires a computational hardness assumption.

## A complete toy exchange

Let us deliberately choose parameters that are tiny enough to inspect manually:

$$
p=23,
\qquad
g=5.
$$

We work in the multiplicative group

$$
\mathbb Z_{23}^{*}.
$$

Because $23$ is prime,

$$
\left|\mathbb Z_{23}^{*}\right|
=
23-1
=
22.
$$

For this particular example, $5$ has order $22$, so it generates the complete multiplicative group:

$$
\langle 5\rangle
=
\mathbb Z_{23}^{*}.
$$

Alice chooses

$$
a=6,
$$

while Bob chooses

$$
b=15.
$$

Alice computes

$$
A
=
5^6\bmod 23
=
8.
$$

Bob computes

$$
B
=
5^{15}\bmod 23
=
19.
$$

These two values are public.

Now Alice computes

$$
Z_A
=
19^6\bmod 23
=
2.
$$

Bob computes

$$
Z_B
=
8^{15}\bmod 23
=
2.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
Z_A=Z_B=2
}
$$

as predicted by the algebra.

We can reproduce the entire protocol in a few lines of Python:

```python
p = 23
g = 5

alice_secret = 6
bob_secret = 15

A = pow(g, alice_secret, p)
B = pow(g, bob_secret, p)

alice_shared = pow(B, alice_secret, p)
bob_shared = pow(A, bob_secret, p)

print(A)             # 8
print(B)             # 19
print(alice_shared)  # 2
print(bob_shared)    # 2

assert alice_shared == bob_shared
```

At this point it would be tempting to stop and say:

> Alice and Bob have computed the same secret. The protocol works.

Mathematically, yes.

Cryptographically, however, we have not asked the adversarial question yet.

## Breaking the toy protocol

Suppose Eve observes the entire public exchange.

She knows

$$
p=23,
\qquad
g=5,
\qquad
A=8,
\qquad
B=19.
$$

She does **not** know

$$
a=6
$$

or

$$
b=15.
$$

Recovering $a$ from

$$
A=g^a
$$

is an instance of the **discrete logarithm problem**.

For our concrete values, Eve wants to solve

$$
5^a\equiv8\pmod{23}.
$$

In a suitably chosen cryptographic group, this should be computationally infeasible.

Our group, however, contains only $22$ elements.

There is no reason to use sophisticated cryptanalysis.

Eve can simply try every candidate exponent.

```python
def brute_force_discrete_log(g, target, p):
    for candidate in range(p - 1):
        if pow(g, candidate, p) == target:
            return candidate

    return None
```

Run:

```python
recovered_a = brute_force_discrete_log(
    g=5,
    target=8,
    p=23,
)

print(recovered_a)
```

Output:

```text
6
```

Alice's secret exponent has been recovered.

Eve can now compute exactly what Alice computes:

```python
recovered_shared = pow(B, recovered_a, p)

print(recovered_shared)
```

Output:

```text
2
```

So Eve obtains

$$
\boxed{
Z=2
}
$$

as well.

Our protocol is therefore:

```text
mathematically correct    ✓

cryptographically secure  ✗
```

Nothing in the algebra failed.

Our parameters failed to make the relevant computational problem difficult.

## What exactly did we break?

It is worth being precise here because several closely related hardness assumptions appear around Diffie-Hellman.

### Discrete logarithm problem

Given

$$
g
$$

and

$$
A=g^a,
$$

recover

$$
a.
$$

Symbolically:

$$
(g,g^a)
\longrightarrow
a.
$$

This is the **discrete logarithm problem**, or DLP.

That is exactly what our brute-force code solved.

### Computational Diffie-Hellman problem

Diffie-Hellman key agreement itself gives the attacker

$$
g,
\qquad
g^a,
\qquad
g^b,
$$

and asks whether the attacker can compute

$$
g^{ab}.
$$

This is the **Computational Diffie-Hellman problem**, or CDH:

$$
(g,g^a,g^b)
\longrightarrow
g^{ab}.
$$

If Eve can solve the discrete logarithm problem, then she can certainly solve CDH:

$$
g^a
\longrightarrow
a
\longrightarrow
(g^b)^a
=
g^{ab}.
$$

So breaking DLP is sufficient to break the toy exchange.

But conceptually the two problems are not identical.

Diffie-Hellman security is more directly connected to the difficulty of computing

$$
g^{ab}
$$

from the public transcript.

Later we will also encounter the **Decisional Diffie-Hellman** problem, which asks whether an attacker can distinguish

$$
g^{ab}
$$

from an unrelated random group element.

For now the hierarchy of ideas is enough:

```text
DLP
recover an exponent

CDH
compute the shared group element

DDH
distinguish the shared group element
from random
```

These distinctions become important when we study formal security definitions and specific protocols built from Diffie-Hellman.

## Why parameter choice matters

Our attack succeeded because the search space was tiny.

Trying approximately $22$ possible exponents is trivial.

It would therefore be wrong to conclude:

> "Diffie-Hellman is insecure because we recovered the exponent."

What we actually showed is:

> **Diffie-Hellman instantiated in a tiny group is insecure because its hardness assumption is false at that scale.**

Security depends on more than the numerical size of $p$.

We need to think about:

- the modulus,
- the order of the group,
- the order of the generator,
- the subgroup actually generated by $g$,
- the best known algorithms against that group.

This is why the earlier discussion of

```text
modulus size

group order

element order

generated subgroup
```

was not abstract algebra for its own sake.

Those quantities directly affect security.

For example, if the group order factors as

$$
q=q_1q_2\cdots q_k
$$

with many small factors, the **Pohlig-Hellman algorithm** can reduce the discrete logarithm problem to discrete logarithms in the corresponding smaller-order components.

So saying only

> "the modulus is large"

is not enough.

For real finite-field Diffie-Hellman, standardized parameter sets are carefully designed so that the relevant subgroup has appropriately large order and the known discrete-logarithm attacks remain computationally infeasible.

Later articles will examine these attacks individually.

## A shared group element is not yet a session key

Our toy program calls

$$
Z=g^{ab}
$$

the shared secret.

That terminology is useful while learning the algebra.

But a real protocol normally should **not** take the raw serialized Diffie-Hellman result and immediately use it as, for example, an AES key.

Instead, the shared secret material is passed through an appropriate **key derivation function** together with protocol context.

Conceptually:

```text
Diffie-Hellman result Z
        ↓
encode Z
        ↓
KDF
        +
protocol context / transcript
        ↓
session key material
```

A simplified abstract form might be

$$
K
=
\operatorname{KDF}
(
Z,
\text{context}
).
$$

The context may bind information such as:

- protocol identifiers,
- participant identities,
- public keys,
- transcript values,
- algorithm choices,
- session labels.

A key derivation function serves several purposes.

It converts the raw shared material into appropriately sized keys, provides domain separation, and can derive multiple independent keys for different purposes.

For example:

```text
shared DH value
      ↓
     KDF
      ↓
+---------------+---------------+
|               |               |
encryption key  authentication  other key material
                key
```

So a more precise statement is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Diffie-Hellman produces shared secret material}
}
$$

from which a protocol derives its actual session keys.

Diffie-Hellman itself is a **key-agreement mechanism**.

It is not bulk encryption.

## Correct does not mean authenticated

There is still a more serious protocol-level problem.

Our exchange assumes that when Alice receives

$$
B,
$$

that value really came from Bob.

And when Bob receives

$$
A,
$$

he assumes it really came from Alice.

But nothing in the basic Diffie-Hellman equations authenticates either participant.

Suppose Eve controls the network.

Instead of trying to recover $a$ or $b$, she can replace the public values.

Alice sends:

$$
A=g^a.
$$

Eve intercepts it and sends her own value

$$
E_1=g^{e_1}
$$

to Bob.

Bob sends:

$$
B=g^b.
$$

Eve intercepts that too and sends

$$
E_2=g^{e_2}
$$

to Alice.

Alice then computes a valid Diffie-Hellman secret:

$$
Z_{AE}
=
E_2^a
=
g^{ae_2}.
$$

Bob independently computes another valid secret:

$$
Z_{BE}
=
E_1^b
=
g^{be_1}.
$$

Eve knows $e_1$ and $e_2$, so she can compute both.

Nothing about the Diffie-Hellman algebra has failed.

The problem is **authentication**.

This gives us another important distinction:

$$
\boxed{
\text{key agreement}
\neq
\text{authenticated key agreement}
}
$$

That distinction is the subject of the next article.

## A compact security picture

At this point we can summarize the toy protocol in four layers.

### Correctness

Alice and Bob agree because

$$
(g^b)^a
=
(g^a)^b
=
g^{ab}.
$$

### Computational security

A passive attacker should not be able to derive the shared value efficiently from

$$
g,
\qquad
g^a,
\qquad
g^b.
$$

This leads to discrete-logarithm and Diffie-Hellman hardness assumptions.

### Parameter security

The chosen group must make the best known attacks computationally infeasible.

A mathematically valid group can still be cryptographically useless.

### Protocol security

Even a hard Diffie-Hellman instance does not authenticate the communicating parties.

An active attacker can manipulate the exchange unless authentication is added.

This is an important progression:

```text
algebra
  ↓
correctness
  ↓
hardness assumption
  ↓
secure parameters
  ↓
protocol composition
  ↓
authentication
```

Cryptography lives at all of these layers simultaneously.

## Run the experiment yourself

Reproduce the exchange from scratch.

Start with:

```text
p = 23

g = 5

a = 6

b = 15
```

Then:

1. compute $A=g^a\bmod p$,
2. compute $B=g^b\bmod p$,
3. compute Alice's shared value,
4. compute Bob's shared value,
5. assert that they are equal,
6. give Eve only $p,g,A,B$,
7. brute-force one secret exponent,
8. recover the shared value.

Then start changing things.

Try a different Alice secret.

Try a different Bob secret.

Try another small prime.

Try another generator.

Then ask:

> What information is public?

> What information is secret?

> Which computational problem is the attacker forced to solve?

> Which assumption would fail if the group were chosen badly?

This attacker-oriented way of reading a protocol is just as important as understanding the correctness proof.

## Papers and standards

### Original Diffie-Hellman paper

**Whitfield Diffie and Martin E. Hellman**,  
*New Directions in Cryptography*,  
IEEE Transactions on Information Theory, Vol. 22, No. 6, pp. 644–654, 1976.

This is the original paper that introduced the key-agreement idea that now carries their names.

[Read the original paper from Stanford](https://ee.stanford.edu/~hellman/publications/24.pdf)

At this point in the series, the paper is especially interesting because we now have enough algebraic vocabulary to recognize the mechanism rather than treating

$$
g^{ab}
$$

as a mysterious final formula.

### Modern finite-field Diffie-Hellman groups

**D. Gillmor**,  
*Negotiated Finite Field Diffie-Hellman Ephemeral Parameters for Transport Layer Security (TLS)*,  
RFC 7919, 2016.

RFC 7919 specifies standardized finite-field Diffie-Hellman groups for TLS and is a useful example of the difference between our toy group and parameters intended for real systems.

### Key-establishment guidance

**NIST SP 800-56A Rev. 3**,  
*Recommendation for Pair-Wise Key-Establishment Schemes Using Discrete Logarithm Cryptography*, 2018.

This document provides a much more formal treatment of finite-field and elliptic-curve discrete-logarithm key-establishment schemes, validation, key derivation, and protocol requirements.

### Key derivation

**H. Krawczyk and P. Eronen**,  
*HMAC-based Extract-and-Expand Key Derivation Function (HKDF)*,  
RFC 5869, 2010.

HKDF is a useful concrete reference for understanding why raw Diffie-Hellman shared material and application session keys should be treated as different objects.

Both sides may still execute perfectly valid Diffie-Hellman computations — just not with each other.
