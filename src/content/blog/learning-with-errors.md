**Learning With Errors (LWE)** asks us to recover — or merely distinguish information about — a secret hidden inside many slightly corrupted linear equations modulo \(q\).

Without the errors, we have ordinary modular linear algebra:

$$
b=As\pmod q,
$$

and Gaussian elimination recovers \(s\) whenever enough independent equations are available.

LWE changes this to

$$
b=As+e\pmod q,
$$

where the error vector \(e\) is small.

That apparently minor modification changes the computational problem completely.

The central idea is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{linear equations}
+
\text{small unknown noise}
\Longrightarrow
\text{lattice hardness}
}
$$

This simple construction became one of the main foundations of modern lattice-based and post-quantum cryptography.

## 1. One LWE sample

Fix:

* a modulus \(q\);
* a dimension \(n\);
* a secret

$$
s\in\mathbb Z_q^n;
$$

* an error distribution \(\chi\) concentrated around small values.

An LWE sample is generated by choosing

$$
a\leftarrow\mathbb Z_q^n
$$

uniformly at random and independently sampling

$$
e\leftarrow\chi.
$$

We then compute

$$
b
=
\langle a,s\rangle+e
\pmod q.
$$

The output is the pair

$$
(a,b).
$$

![Noisy linear equations in LWE](/images/blog/lattices/lwe1.png)

For a fixed secret \(s\), the resulting distribution is often denoted

$$
A_{s,\chi}.
$$

So conceptually,

$$
A_{s,\chi}
:
\qquad
a\leftarrow\mathbb Z_q^n,
\qquad
e\leftarrow\chi,
\qquad
(a,\langle a,s\rangle+e\bmod q).
$$

The error is small as an **integer**, even though the equation itself is evaluated modulo \(q\).

That distinction is crucial.

### What does “small” mean?

Suppose \(q=3329\).

An error such as

$$
e=2
$$

is tiny relative to the modulus.

An error such as

$$
e=1600
$$

is not.

The correct scale therefore depends on \(q\).

In theoretical LWE, the noise is often described using a parameter

$$
\alpha
$$

so that the typical error magnitude is related to

$$
\alpha q.
$$

Gaussian or discrete-Gaussian-like error distributions are common in hardness theory, while practical schemes often use carefully chosen small discrete distributions.

The important principle is not merely

$$
e\neq0,
$$

but rather

$$
e
\text{ is random, hidden, and correctly scaled}.
$$

## 2. A tiny numerical example

Take

$$
q=17
$$

and let the secret be

$$
s=
\begin{pmatrix}
4\\
7
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Choose

$$
a_1=
\begin{pmatrix}
3\\
5
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Without noise,

$$
\langle a_1,s\rangle
=
3\cdot4+5\cdot7
=
47.
$$

Modulo \(17\),

$$
47\equiv13\pmod{17}.
$$

Now choose the small error

$$
e_1=1.
$$

Then the LWE value is

$$
b_1=14.
$$

So the attacker sees

$$
\left(
\begin{pmatrix}
3\\
5
\end{pmatrix},
14
\right)
$$

rather than the exact equation

$$
3s_1+5s_2\equiv13\pmod{17}.
$$

Take a second sample:

$$
a_2=
\begin{pmatrix}
8\\
2
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
e_2=-1.
$$

Then

$$
8\cdot4+2\cdot7
=
46
\equiv12\pmod{17},
$$

so

$$
b_2=11.
$$

A third:

$$
a_3=
\begin{pmatrix}
6\\
9
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
e_3=2.
$$

We obtain

$$
6\cdot4+9\cdot7
=
87
\equiv2\pmod{17},
$$

and therefore

$$
b_3=4.
$$

The attacker observes

$$
\begin{aligned}
3s_1+5s_2 &\approx 14 \pmod{17},\\
8s_1+2s_2 &\approx 11 \pmod{17},\\
6s_1+9s_2 &\approx 4 \pmod{17}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The symbol

$$
\approx
$$

is informal here.

Each equation is not merely approximate over the real numbers. Rather, there exists a small hidden integer \(e_i\) such that

$$
b_i-\langle a_i,s\rangle
\equiv e_i\pmod q.
$$

Without those errors, ordinary modular elimination would reveal \(s\).

With them, none of the equations is exactly correct.

## 3. Many samples and matrix notation

Suppose we collect \(m\) independent LWE samples

$$
(a_i,b_i),
\qquad
i=1,\ldots,m.
$$

Let the rows of

$$
A\in\mathbb Z_q^{m\times n}
$$

be

$$
a_i^T.
$$

Define

$$
b=
\begin{pmatrix}
b_1\\
\vdots\\
b_m
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
e=
\begin{pmatrix}
e_1\\
\vdots\\
e_m
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Then all equations can be written compactly as

$$
\boxed{
b=As+e\pmod q.
}
$$

This is the basic LWE equation.

Compare it with the noiseless system

$$
b=As\pmod q.
$$

The only difference is \(e\).

Yet that small vector is precisely what prevents us from directly applying modular Gaussian elimination.

### Why can we not simply “subtract the error”?

Because the attacker does not know \(e\).

They know only that its coordinates were sampled from some specified distribution concentrated around zero.

Thus both

$$
s
$$

and

$$
e
$$

are unknown.

The system therefore mixes an algebraic unknown with a constrained geometric unknown.

That combination is where the lattice structure enters.

## 4. Search-LWE

The **Search Learning With Errors problem** asks us to recover the secret.

Informally:

> Given sufficiently many independent samples
>
> $$
> (a_i,b_i)
> $$
>
> satisfying
>
> $$
> b_i
> =
> \langle a_i,s\rangle+e_i
> \pmod q,
> $$
>
> recover
>
> $$
> s\in\mathbb Z_q^n.
> $$

In matrix form:

$$
A\leftarrow\mathbb Z_q^{m\times n},
$$

$$
e\leftarrow\chi^m,
$$

$$
b=As+e\pmod q.
$$

The computational task is

$$
(A,b)
\longmapsto
s.
$$

### Compare with ordinary linear algebra

If

$$
e=0,
$$

then

$$
b=As\pmod q.
$$

If \(A\) contains enough independent rows, recovering \(s\) is an ordinary linear-algebra problem.

With LWE,

$$
b_i-\langle a_i,s\rangle
$$

is not zero.

It is merely expected to be small modulo \(q\).

So the system is deliberately inconsistent as an exact modular linear system.

That is precisely the point.

## 5. Decision-LWE

In cryptography, we often do not need an attacker to recover \(s\).

It is enough to require that the LWE samples look random.

This leads to **Decision-LWE**.

The challenger produces one of two distributions.

### LWE world

Choose

$$
s\leftarrow\mathbb Z_q^n,
$$

$$
A\leftarrow\mathbb Z_q^{m\times n},
$$

$$
e\leftarrow\chi^m,
$$

and output

$$
(A,As+e).
$$

### Uniform world

Choose

$$
A\leftarrow\mathbb Z_q^{m\times n}
$$

and independently choose

$$
u\leftarrow\mathbb Z_q^m.
$$

Output

$$
(A,u).
$$

The Decision-LWE problem asks:

> Can an efficient algorithm distinguish these two distributions with non-negligible advantage?

Symbolically,

$$
(A,As+e)
\stackrel{?}{\approx_c}
(A,u),
$$

where

$$
\approx_c
$$

denotes computational indistinguishability.

![Search/decision intuition for LWE](/images/blog/lattices/lwe2.png)

The distinction is important:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Search-LWE}
:
\text{recover }s
}
$$

whereas

$$
\boxed{
\text{Decision-LWE}
:
\text{recognize that LWE structure exists}
}
$$

without necessarily learning \(s\).

This second formulation is especially useful for encryption security because cryptographic proofs often want to replace structured public data with uniformly random data through a hybrid argument.

## 6. Search versus decision

It is tempting to say:

> Search-LWE and Decision-LWE are equivalent.

That statement needs hypotheses.

For standard parameter settings, powerful reductions relate the two problems, but the exact theorem depends on:

* the modulus \(q\);
* the distribution of the secret;
* the error distribution;
* the number of samples;
* the exact LWE variant.

A particularly clean classical reduction for standard LWE works when \(q\) is prime and polynomially bounded.

The basic idea is surprisingly elegant.

Suppose we have a distinguisher capable of determining whether samples are LWE samples or uniform.

To recover the first secret coordinate \(s_1\), we can guess

$$
k\in\mathbb Z_q.
$$

For a sample

$$
(a,b),
$$

choose random

$$
r\in\mathbb Z_q
$$

and transform it approximately as

$$
a'
=
a+r e_1,
$$

$$
b'
=
b+r k,
$$

where \(e_1\) here denotes the first standard basis vector.

If

$$
k=s_1,
$$

the transformed distribution retains the correct LWE structure.

If

$$
k\neq s_1,
$$

the transformation can destroy that structure and, under the appropriate hypotheses, make the relevant component uniform.

The decision oracle can therefore test guesses for \(s_1\).

Repeating this coordinate by coordinate recovers \(s\).

This is a reduction of the form

$$
\boxed{
\text{Decision-LWE solver}
\Longrightarrow
\text{Search-LWE solver}
}
$$

under the assumptions of the theorem.

The broader lesson is more important than the particular proof:

> distinguishing noisy linear equations from randomness can be computationally powerful enough to recover the hidden secret itself.

But this equivalence should always be attached to its parameter assumptions rather than treated as a universal identity between all possible LWE variants.

## 7. The q-ary lattice viewpoint

Now the geometry from the previous SIS chapter returns.

Let

$$
A\in\mathbb Z^{m\times n}
$$

be an integer representative of our modular matrix.

Define the q-ary lattice

$$
\Lambda_q(A)
=
\left\{
As+qz:
s\in\mathbb Z^n,
\;
z\in\mathbb Z^m
\right\}.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\Lambda_q(A)
=
A\mathbb Z^n+q\mathbb Z^m.
$$

Consider an LWE vector

$$
b=As+e\pmod q.
$$

Choose an integer lift

$$
\tilde b\in\mathbb Z^m
$$

of \(b\).

Then for some

$$
z\in\mathbb Z^m,
$$

we have

$$
\tilde b
=
As+e+qz.
$$

Therefore,

$$
As+qz
\in
\Lambda_q(A),
$$

and

$$
\tilde b-(As+qz)=e.
$$

So \(\tilde b\) lies close to a lattice point:

$$
\boxed{
\tilde b
=
v+e,
\qquad
v\in\Lambda_q(A).
}
$$

![SIS and LWE as related q-ary lattice pictures](/images/blog/lattices/sisvslwe.png)

This gives a beautiful geometric interpretation:

$$
\boxed{
\text{LWE sample vector}
=
\text{q-ary lattice point}
+
\text{small perturbation}.
}
$$

In that sense, recovering the hidden structure resembles a decoding problem.

## 8. LWE and bounded-distance decoding

Recall the **Bounded Distance Decoding problem (BDD)**.

Given a lattice

$$
\Lambda
$$

and a target point

$$
t
$$

promised to lie sufficiently close to \(\Lambda\), find the nearby lattice point.

Formally, if

$$
t=v+e,
\qquad
v\in\Lambda,
$$

with sufficiently small \(e\), recover \(v\).

The LWE equation has exactly this visual form:

$$
\tilde b=v+e.
$$

So LWE has a natural BDD interpretation.

However, we should be precise.

It is misleading to write

$$
\text{LWE}=\text{BDD}.
$$

LWE instances have very specific additional structure:

* the lattice is a random q-ary lattice;
* the target is generated through modular linear equations;
* the secret has a specified distribution;
* the noise has a specified distribution;
* the attacker receives samples generated according to the LWE experiment.

Thus BDD is best understood as part of the **geometric mechanism behind LWE hardness reductions**, not as a complete definition of LWE itself.

The conceptual path is

$$
\boxed{
\text{noisy modular equations}
\rightarrow
\text{target near a q-ary lattice}
\rightarrow
\text{decoding geometry}.
}
$$

## 9. Worst-case to average-case hardness

This is the deepest theoretical reason LWE became so important.

An LWE instance is random:

$$
A\leftarrow\mathbb Z_q^{m\times n},
$$

and the errors are randomly sampled.

So LWE is naturally an **average-case problem**.

By contrast, classical lattice problems such as

$$
\operatorname{GapSVP}_\gamma
$$

and

$$
\operatorname{SIVP}_\gamma
$$

are worst-case problems.

They ask for an algorithm that succeeds on arbitrary lattices.

Regev's breakthrough established a reduction connecting these two worlds.

At a high level,

$$
\boxed{
\text{efficient algorithm for suitable LWE}
}
$$

would imply

$$
\boxed{
\text{efficient algorithms for approximate lattice problems}
}
$$

on arbitrary lattices.

For standard parameterizations of the original result, the reduction relates LWE to approximate versions of problems such as

$$
\operatorname{GapSVP}
$$

and

$$
\operatorname{SIVP}.
$$

The approximation factor depends on the LWE parameters and is often expressed asymptotically in terms related to

$$
\widetilde O(n/\alpha),
$$

where \(\alpha\) controls the relative noise magnitude.

A typical technical condition appearing in this line of results is that the noise scale satisfies roughly

$$
\alpha q
\gtrsim
\sqrt n.
$$

The exact constants and parameter conditions belong to the particular reduction theorem.

### The original reduction is quantum

A historically important detail is that Regev's original worst-case-to-average-case reduction uses a quantum procedure.

In simplified form, the proof connects several objects:

$$
\text{worst-case lattice problem}
\rightarrow
\text{Gaussian sampling}
\rightarrow
\text{BDD}
\rightarrow
\text{LWE}.
$$

The quantum part appears in a procedure involving Gaussian states and the quantum Fourier transform.

Therefore, the original theorem does **not** say merely:

> LWE is classically equivalent to worst-case SVP.

That would be incorrect.

Instead, the implication is of the form

$$
\boxed{
\text{efficient classical LWE solver}
\Longrightarrow
\text{efficient quantum algorithm for certain worst-case lattice problems}.
}
$$

This is already extremely significant for post-quantum cryptography.

It means that if an attacker — even a classical one — could efficiently solve those LWE instances, then the attacker would indirectly yield a quantum solution to worst-case lattice problems that are believed to remain difficult.

### Classical hardness results

Later work, particularly by Peikert and others, developed classical reductions for important variants and parameter regimes.

These results strengthened the theoretical understanding of LWE considerably.

But it is still better to say

> LWE has both quantum and classical worst-case hardness reductions under different parameter settings and assumptions,

rather than claiming that one single theorem gives an unconditional classical equivalence between every form of LWE and ordinary SVP.

### What the theorem does not mean

None of the following statements is accurate without major qualification:

$$
\text{LWE}=\text{SVP},
$$

or

$$
\text{breaking one LWE instance solves exact SVP},
$$

or

$$
\text{every possible choice of LWE parameters has a worst-case reduction}.
$$

The correct conceptual message is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{carefully parameterized average-case LWE}
\text{ inherits hardness evidence from}
\text{ worst-case lattice problems}.
}
$$

This worst-case/average-case bridge is one of the defining theoretical achievements of lattice cryptography.

## 10. Regev-style public-key encryption

LWE can be turned directly into public-key encryption.

The following pedagogical construction exposes the core mechanism particularly clearly.

Let

$$
A\in\mathbb Z_q^{m\times n}.
$$

### Key generation

Choose a secret

$$
s\leftarrow\mathbb Z_q^n.
$$

Generate a random matrix

$$
A\leftarrow\mathbb Z_q^{m\times n}.
$$

Sample a small error vector

$$
e\leftarrow\chi^m.
$$

Compute

$$
b=As+e\pmod q.
$$

The public key is

$$
\boxed{
pk=(A,b)
}
$$

and the secret key is

$$
\boxed{
sk=s.
}
$$

Notice what the public key actually contains:

$$
(A,b)
=
(A,As+e).
$$

It is simply a collection of LWE samples.

---

### Encrypt one bit

Suppose

$$
\mu\in\{0,1\}.
$$

Choose

$$
r\leftarrow\{0,1\}^m.
$$

Think of \(r\) as selecting a random subset of the rows of the public key.

Compute

$$
u=A^Tr\pmod q
$$

and

$$
v
=
b^Tr
+
\mu
\left\lfloor
\frac q2
\right\rfloor
\pmod q.
$$

The ciphertext is

$$
\boxed{
c=(u,v).
}
$$

For

$$
\mu=0,
$$

the message is encoded near

$$
0.
$$

For

$$
\mu=1,
$$

it is encoded near

$$
\frac q2.
$$

The two message values are therefore separated by approximately half the modulus.

---

### Decryption

The secret-key holder computes

$$
d
=
v-\langle u,s\rangle
\pmod q.
$$

Substitute

$$
u=A^Tr
$$

and

$$
v=b^Tr+
\mu
\left\lfloor
\frac q2
\right\rfloor.
$$

Then

$$
d
=
b^Tr
-
\langle A^Tr,s\rangle
+
\mu
\left\lfloor
\frac q2
\right\rfloor.
$$

Since

$$
\langle A^Tr,s\rangle
=
r^TAs,
$$

we obtain

$$
d
=
r^T(b-As)
+
\mu
\left\lfloor
\frac q2
\right\rfloor.
$$

But

$$
b-As=e.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
d
=
e^Tr
+
\mu
\left\lfloor
\frac q2
\right\rfloor
\pmod q.
}
$$

All dependence on the secret has cancelled.

What remains is only:

1. the encoded message;
2. accumulated small noise.

That cancellation is the heart of the scheme.

---

## 11. Why decryption works

Suppose first that

$$
\mu=0.
$$

Then

$$
d=e^Tr.
$$

If the accumulated error is small,

$$
d
$$

lies close to

$$
0.
$$

Now suppose

$$
\mu=1.
$$

Then

$$
d
=
e^Tr+
\left\lfloor\frac q2\right\rfloor.
$$

So \(d\) lies close to

$$
q/2.
$$

Therefore the decoder asks:

> Is \(d\) closer modulo \(q\) to \(0\), or to \(q/2\)?

Ignoring minor rounding effects when \(q\) is odd, correctness is guaranteed whenever

$$
|e^Tr|<\frac q4.
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{
|e^Tr|<q/4
\Longrightarrow
\text{correct decryption}.
}
$$

### Why the total noise grows

Because

$$
r_i\in\{0,1\},
$$

we have

$$
e^Tr
=
\sum_{i:r_i=1} e_i.
$$

So encryption combines several LWE errors.

The effective ciphertext noise is therefore larger than the noise in a single public-key sample.

This is why parameter selection must analyze the **distribution of the accumulated noise**, not only the magnitude of one error coefficient.

If the individual errors are roughly independent and centered, their standard deviations combine approximately according to a square-root law rather than simply adding worst-case magnitudes.

But for a correctness proof, one must still bound the probability that

$$
|e^Tr|
$$

crosses the decoding threshold.

## 12. Why the encryption is secure

Correctness and security come from different arguments.

The security intuition begins with Decision-LWE.

The public key is

$$
(A,b)
=
(A,As+e).
$$

If Decision-LWE is hard, an adversary should not efficiently distinguish this from

$$
(A,u),
\qquad
u\leftarrow\mathbb Z_q^m.
$$

So in a proof we can conceptually replace the real public key

$$
(A,As+e)
$$

with an essentially random pair

$$
(A,u).
$$

But one more step is required.

Encryption computes subset sums using

$$
r\in\{0,1\}^m.
$$

For example,

$$
u_c=A^Tr
$$

and

$$
v_c=b^Tr+\mu\lfloor q/2\rfloor.
$$

When the public-key components are uniform and \(m\) provides enough entropy, random subset sums become statistically close to uniform over the appropriate output space.

This is the role of a **leftover-hash-style argument** in the security proof.

Therefore the proof has roughly the form

$$
\boxed{
\text{real LWE public key}
}
$$

$$
\Downarrow
\quad
\text{Decision-LWE}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{uniform-looking public key}
}
$$

$$
\Downarrow
\quad
\text{subset-sum / leftover-hash argument}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{ciphertext hides }\mu.
}
$$

This is much more precise than saying simply:

> the noise hides the message.

The noise first makes the public key computationally pseudorandom.

The randomized subset-selection mechanism then converts that pseudorandom public material into a ciphertext distribution that does not reveal the encrypted bit.

The resulting construction provides the core intuition behind LWE-based public-key encryption.

## 13. Noise and parameter selection

Noise is simultaneously the source of security and the source of possible decryption failure.

This creates a fundamental tension.

### Too little noise

If

$$
e=0,
$$

the system becomes

$$
b=As,
$$

and ordinary modular linear algebra recovers the secret.

More generally, poorly chosen or excessively narrow error distributions may expose additional structure or invalidate the assumptions behind a hardness theorem.

### Too much noise

If the error becomes too large, then during decryption

$$
e^Tr
$$

may cross the decision boundary between

$$
0
$$

and

$$
q/2.
$$

Then the legitimate receiver may decrypt incorrectly.

Thus the parameters must satisfy both

$$
\boxed{
\text{noise large enough for security}
}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{
\text{noise small enough for correctness}.
}
$$

This balancing act involves several interacting parameters:

$$
n,
\qquad
q,
\qquad
m,
\qquad
\chi,
\qquad
\alpha,
\qquad
\text{secret distribution}.
$$

They cannot be chosen independently.

### Security and correctness are different statements

This distinction is important:

$$
\text{correctness}
\neq
\text{security}.
$$

Correctness asks:

> Does the legitimate receiver recover the intended message with overwhelming probability?

Security asks:

> Can an efficient adversary distinguish or recover information that should remain hidden?

A system can be secure but have an unacceptable decryption-failure rate.

It can also decrypt perfectly while being completely insecure.

Both analyses are required.

## 14. Attack families

LWE should not be thought of as being attacked by a single algorithm such as LLL.

Real parameter analysis considers several qualitatively different attack strategies.

### Primal lattice attacks

A primal attack constructs a lattice in which the LWE secret/error information corresponds to an unusually short or otherwise identifiable vector.

The attacker then applies lattice reduction, commonly modeled using algorithms such as BKZ, and possibly enumeration or sieving.

Conceptually,

$$
\text{LWE instance}
\rightarrow
\text{embedded lattice}
\rightarrow
\text{recover short vector}.
$$

---

### Dual lattice attacks

A dual attack attempts to find a short vector \(w\) that creates a useful modular relation among the public samples.

If

$$
w^TA
\equiv0
\pmod q
$$

or is otherwise appropriately structured, then

$$
w^Tb
=
w^TAs+w^Te
$$

can suppress the secret-dependent component and leave a quantity influenced mainly by the small error.

This can create a statistical distinguisher.

So the primal and dual viewpoints are roughly:

$$
\boxed{
\text{primal: recover hidden short structure}
}
$$

versus

$$
\boxed{
\text{dual: find a short relation that exposes bias}.
}
$$

---

### Decoding and embedding attacks

Because LWE has a BDD/CVP interpretation, decoding techniques can be applied after embedding the instance into a suitable lattice.

The problem may be transformed into finding a short vector or a closest vector in a higher-dimensional lattice.

---

### BKW-style attacks

The Blum–Kalai–Wasserman strategy originates in learning parity with noise.

Its descendants eliminate groups of coordinates by combining samples.

Very roughly, one tries to transform

$$
\langle a,s\rangle+e
$$

into samples involving progressively fewer secret coordinates.

The price is that combining samples also combines noise.

Thus BKW-type methods trade

$$
\text{dimension reduction}
$$

against

$$
\text{noise growth}.
$$

---

### Algebraic attacks

For certain unusual parameter choices — particularly very small or highly constrained error supports — LWE equations may be encoded as polynomial systems.

Gröbner-basis and related algebraic techniques can then become relevant.

This is one reason that saying only

> the error is small

is not enough.

Its complete distribution matters.

---

### Guessing and hybrid attacks

If the secret is small or sparse, an attacker may guess part of it and apply a lattice or combinatorial technique to the remainder.

Such methods are called **hybrid attacks**.

A simplified pattern is

$$
\boxed{
\text{guess some coordinates}
+
\text{solve reduced LWE instance}.
}
$$

This is especially relevant when practical schemes use secrets from distributions much smaller than uniform \(\mathbb Z_q^n\).

---

### Concrete security

Modern LWE parameter selection therefore does not rely solely on an asymptotic statement such as

$$
\text{LWE is worst-case hard}.
$$

Instead, one estimates the computational cost of the best known attack families for the exact parameters being proposed.

This includes assumptions about:

* lattice dimension;
* modulus;
* error distribution;
* secret distribution;
* available number of samples;
* BKZ block size;
* sieving or enumeration cost models;
* classical versus quantum cost assumptions.

Hence:

$$
\boxed{
\text{asymptotic reduction}
\neq
\text{concrete security estimate}.
}
$$

Both are useful, but they answer different questions.

## 15. SIS and LWE side by side

The previous chapter introduced SIS:

$$
Az\equiv0\pmod q,
$$

where the goal is to find a short nonzero vector

$$
z.
$$

LWE instead gives

$$
b=As+e\pmod q
$$

and asks us to recover or distinguish hidden information despite the small error.

The two problems therefore emphasize different geometric phenomena.

### SIS

Given

$$
A,
$$

find

$$
z\neq0
$$

such that

$$
Az\equiv0\pmod q
$$

and

$$
\|z\|
\text{ is small}.
$$

Mental model:

$$
\boxed{
\text{find a short modular relation}.
}
$$

### LWE

Given

$$
A
$$

and

$$
b=As+e,
$$

recover \(s\) or distinguish the samples from random.

Mental model:

$$
\boxed{
\text{recover structure hidden by small noise}.
}
$$

The relationship is therefore almost complementary:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\text{SIS}
&:
\text{short relations},
\\[4pt]
\text{LWE}
&:
\text{noisy equations}.
\end{aligned}
}
$$

Both naturally produce q-ary lattice geometry.

Both admit remarkable worst-case-to-average-case hardness results.

And together they underpin a large fraction of modern lattice-based cryptography.

## 16. From LWE to structured LWE

Plain LWE uses an essentially unstructured matrix

$$
A\in\mathbb Z_q^{m\times n}.
$$

This has strong theoretical advantages.

But storing and multiplying by a large dense matrix is expensive.

A natural question is therefore:

> Can we retain the noisy-linear-equation idea while replacing the large matrix by something much more compact?

One solution is to replace vectors and matrices with polynomial arithmetic.

Instead of

$$
a,s,e\in\mathbb Z_q^n,
$$

we work with elements of a quotient ring such as

$$
R_q
=
\mathbb Z_q[x]/(f(x)).
$$

The LWE equation becomes something resembling

$$
b=a\cdot s+e
$$

inside the ring.

This leads to **Ring-LWE**.

A more flexible middle ground groups several ring elements into vectors and matrices.

This leads to **Module-LWE**.

The progression is therefore

$$
\boxed{
\text{LWE}
\rightarrow
\text{Ring-LWE}
\rightarrow
\text{Module-LWE}.
}
$$

The benefit is dramatic:

$$
\text{smaller keys}
+
\text{faster arithmetic}
+
\text{structured polynomial multiplication}.
$$

But structure is never free.

Once algebraic structure is introduced, we must ask:

* what lattice family does the structure correspond to?
* which worst-case hardness theorem still applies?
* what assumptions have changed?
* what new attacks might exploit the structure?

Those questions are the subject of the next chapter.
