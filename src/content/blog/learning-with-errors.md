Learning With Errors (LWE) asks us to recover—or distinguish information about—a secret hidden inside many **slightly corrupted linear equations modulo $q$**. Without the errors, Gaussian elimination solves the system. With carefully sized errors, the same-looking linear algebra becomes the foundation of modern post-quantum cryptography.

## 1. One LWE sample

Fix a modulus $q$, dimension $n$, secret

$$
s\in\mathbb Z_q^n,
$$

and an error distribution $\chi$ concentrated on small integers.

An LWE sample is generated by drawing

$$
a\leftarrow\mathbb Z_q^n,
\qquad
 e\leftarrow\chi,
$$

and returning

$$
(a,b),
\qquad
b=\langle a,s\rangle+e\pmod q.
$$

![Noisy linear equations in LWE](/images/blog/lattices/lwe1.png)

The word “small” must be interpreted relative to $q$. If errors are too large, correctness disappears; if they are too small or otherwise badly distributed, security may collapse.

## 2. Search LWE

Given many independent samples

$$
(a_i,b_i),
\qquad
b_i=\langle a_i,s\rangle+e_i\pmod q,
$$

**Search-LWE** asks for the secret $s$.

In matrix notation, with rows of $A$ equal to the $a_i^T$,

$$
b=As+e\pmod q.
$$

If $e=0$, we have an ordinary modular linear system. The difficulty is therefore created by a tiny amount of uncertainty deliberately injected into each equation.

## 3. Decision LWE

**Decision-LWE** asks us to distinguish

$$
(A,As+e)
$$

from a uniformly random pair

$$
(A,u),
\qquad u\leftarrow\mathbb Z_q^m.
$$

![Search/decision intuition for LWE](/images/blog/lattices/lwe2.png)

Search and decision variants are related, but equivalence statements depend on the modulus, secret distribution, error distribution, and the exact theorem being used. They should not be treated as interchangeable without hypotheses.

## 4. The q-ary lattice viewpoint

Consider

$$
\Lambda_q(A)=\{As+qz:s\in\mathbb Z^n,\ z\in\mathbb Z^m\}.
$$

The vector $As$ is a lattice point and

$$
b=As+e
$$

is nearby. Recovering the nearby lattice point is therefore reminiscent of BDD/CVP.

![SIS and LWE as related q-ary lattice pictures](/images/blog/lattices/sisvslwe.png)

This geometric interpretation is useful, but one should not collapse all LWE theorems into “LWE equals CVP.” LWE has a specific random modular structure, and its hardness theory is more subtle.

## 5. Worst-case to average-case hardness

Regev's breakthrough connected average-case LWE to worst-case approximate lattice problems. The original reduction used quantum computation; later work established classical reductions for important parameter regimes and related formulations.

The conceptual statement is the important one:

> An algorithm that reliably solves suitable random LWE instances would imply algorithms for hard approximate problems on arbitrary lattices.

The exact approximation factor depends on the parameters and theorem. This is stronger—and more precise—than saying merely that “LWE is as hard as SVP.”

## 6. Regev-style public-key encryption

A useful pedagogical form of LWE encryption exposes the core idea.

### Key generation

Choose

$$
A\leftarrow\mathbb Z_q^{m\times n},
\qquad
s\leftarrow\mathbb Z_q^n,
\qquad
e\leftarrow\chi^m,
$$

and define

$$
b=As+e\pmod q.
$$

The public key is $(A,b)$ and the secret key is $s$.

### Encrypt one bit

For $\mu\in\{0,1\}$ choose a short binary selector

$$
r\leftarrow\{0,1\}^m.
$$

Compute

$$
u=A^Tr\pmod q,
$$

and

$$
v=b^Tr+\mu\left\lfloor\frac q2\right\rfloor\pmod q.
$$

The ciphertext is $(u,v)$.

### Decryption

Compute

$$
v-\langle u,s\rangle
=
 e^Tr+
\mu\left\lfloor\frac q2\right\rfloor
\pmod q.
$$

If the accumulated error $e^Tr$ remains much smaller than $q/4$, the result is closer to $0$ for $\mu=0$ and closer to $q/2$ for $\mu=1$.

## 7. Correctness and security are different constraints

The same noise plays two roles:

- **security:** it hides the exact linear relation;
- **correctness:** it must remain small enough that the encoded message can still be decoded.

Cryptographic parameter selection is therefore a balancing act between dimension, modulus, sample count, secret distribution, and error width.

## 8. Attack families

LWE is not attacked only by “running LLL.” Important families include:

- primal lattice attacks;
- dual lattice attacks;
- decoding/embedding attacks;
- BKW-style combinatorial attacks;
- algebraic attacks for special or tiny error supports;
- hybrid attacks that guess part of the secret and reduce the remainder.

Serious parameter selection therefore relies on concrete attack estimates, not on a single asymptotic hardness slogan.

## 9. From LWE to structured LWE

Plain LWE uses large unstructured matrices. Modern systems often replace matrix structure with polynomial or module structure to obtain much smaller keys and faster arithmetic.

That leads to **Ring-LWE** and **Module-LWE**, the subject of the next chapter.
