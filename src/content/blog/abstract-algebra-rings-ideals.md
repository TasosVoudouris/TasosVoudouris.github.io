Groups describe one operation. Rings are the first setting in which addition and multiplication interact.

## 1. Rings and conventions

A **ring** $(R,+,\cdot)$ has an abelian group $(R,+)$, an associative multiplication, and the distributive laws
$$
a(b+c)=ab+ac,\qquad (a+b)c=ac+bc.
$$

In CryptoCave we use the common algebra/number-theory convention that rings have a multiplicative identity $1$. Some texts allow rings without $1$, so definitions should always be checked when comparing sources.

A ring is **commutative** if $ab=ba$ for all $a,b$.

Examples include
$$
\mathbb Z,\quad \mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z,\quad F[x],\quad \mathbb Z[i].
$$

## 2. Units and zero divisors

A **unit** is an element $u\in R$ that has a multiplicative inverse. The units form a group $R^\times$.

A nonzero element $a$ is a **zero divisor** if there exists nonzero $b$ such that $ab=0$.

A commutative ring with $1\ne0$ and no zero divisors is an **integral domain**.

Thus $\mathbb Z$ is an integral domain, while $\mathbb Z/15\mathbb Z$ is not because
$$
3\cdot5\equiv0\pmod{15}.
$$

## 3. Characteristic

The characteristic of a ring is the least positive $n$ such that
$$
n\cdot1_R=0,
$$
if such an $n$ exists; otherwise the characteristic is $0$.

For a field, the characteristic is either $0$ or a prime number. If a field had composite characteristic $ab$, then $(a\cdot1)(b\cdot1)=0$ would create zero divisors.

## 4. Ring homomorphisms

A ring homomorphism preserves both operations and the identity under our convention:
$$
\varphi(a+b)=\varphi(a)+\varphi(b),\qquad
\varphi(ab)=\varphi(a)\varphi(b),\qquad
\varphi(1_R)=1_S.
$$

Its kernel is not merely a subgroup: it is an **ideal**.

## 5. Ideals

For a commutative ring $R$, a subset $I\subseteq R$ is an ideal if

1. $(I,+)$ is an additive subgroup of $(R,+)$;
2. $ri\in I$ for all $r\in R$ and $i\in I$.

The prototype is
$$
n\mathbb Z=\{nk:k\in\mathbb Z\}.
$$

The ideal generated by $a_1,\dots,a_m$ is written
$$
(a_1,\dots,a_m).
$$

## 6. Quotient rings

If $I\triangleleft R$, the quotient ring
$$
R/I
$$
consists of additive cosets $r+I$. Addition and multiplication are inherited:
$$
(r+I)+(s+I)=(r+s)+I,
$$
$$
(r+I)(s+I)=rs+I.
$$

The familiar ring $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$ is exactly $\mathbb Z/(n)$.

## 7. Prime and maximal ideals

Two ideal classes explain when quotients become domains or fields:

- $P$ is **prime** iff $R/P$ is an integral domain;
- $M$ is **maximal** iff $R/M$ is a field.

For example, $(p)$ is maximal in $\mathbb Z$ exactly when $p$ is prime, giving the field $\mathbb F_p$.

## 8. Why ideals are central

The same quotient mechanism constructs extension fields:
$$
\mathbb F_p[x]/(f(x)),
$$
where $f$ is irreducible. It also underlies coordinate rings in algebraic geometry and many quotient structures used in modern cryptography.
