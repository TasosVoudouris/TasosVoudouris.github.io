Fields are the setting in which addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division by nonzero elements are all available. They support linear algebra, polynomial factorization, finite-field cryptography, and the coordinate arithmetic of elliptic curves.

## 1. Fields

A **field** $F$ is a commutative ring with $1\ne0$ in which every nonzero element is a unit. Equivalently,
$$
(F,+)\text{ is an abelian group},
$$
and
$$
(F^\times,\cdot)\text{ is an abelian group}.
$$

Examples are $\mathbb Q$, $\mathbb R$, $\mathbb C$, and $\mathbb F_p$ for prime $p$.

## 2. Extension fields

If $F\subseteq E$ and the operations of $F$ agree with those inherited from $E$, then $E/F$ is a **field extension**.

The field $E$ is a vector space over $F$. Its dimension is the extension degree
$$
[E:F].
$$

For example,
$$
\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)=\{a+b\sqrt2:a,b\in\mathbb Q\}
$$
has degree $2$ over $\mathbb Q$.

## 3. Algebraic and transcendental elements

An element $\alpha\in E$ is **algebraic over $F$** if some nonzero polynomial $f\in F[x]$ satisfies $f(\alpha)=0$. Otherwise it is transcendental.

Among all polynomials that vanish at an algebraic $\alpha$, there is a unique monic irreducible one of least degree: the **minimal polynomial** $m_\alpha(x)$.

Then
$$
[F(\alpha):F]=\deg m_\alpha.
$$

## 4. Simple and finite extensions

A field generated by one element is written $F(\alpha)$ and is called a **simple extension**.

Every finite extension is algebraic. The converse is false in general: an algebraic extension can have infinite degree.

The tower law is fundamental:
$$
K\subseteq F\subseteq E
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
[E:K]=[E:F][F:K]
$$
when the degrees are finite.

## 5. Splitting fields

A **splitting field** of $f\in F[x]$ is the smallest extension of $F$ in which $f$ factors completely into linear factors.

For $x^2-2$, the splitting field over $\mathbb Q$ is $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$. For $x^3-2$, adjoining only $\sqrt[3]2$ is not enough to split the polynomial; complex cube roots of unity are also needed.

This distinction matters: *containing one root* and *being a splitting field* are different claims.

## 6. Algebraic closure

A field is **algebraically closed** if every nonconstant polynomial has a root in it. An **algebraic closure** $\overline F$ is an algebraic extension of $F$ that is algebraically closed.

For example,
$$
\overline{\mathbb R}=\mathbb C.
$$
An algebraic closure is unique up to an isomorphism that fixes $F$, but not canonically unique as a concrete set.

## 7. Field automorphisms

An automorphism of $E$ is a bijective field homomorphism $\sigma:E\to E$. If $E/F$ is an extension, the automorphisms fixing $F$ pointwise form
$$
\operatorname{Aut}(E/F).
$$
This is the doorway to Galois theory.

## 8. Perfect fields

A field $F$ is **perfect** if every irreducible polynomial over $F$ is separable. Every characteristic-zero field is perfect. Every finite field is perfect because the Frobenius map
$$
x\mapsto x^p
$$
is an automorphism of a finite field of characteristic $p$.

This correction is more precise than saying merely that “every algebraic extension is separable”: perfection is defined through irreducible polynomials, equivalently through algebraic extensions under the usual formulations.
