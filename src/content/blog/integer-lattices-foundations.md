A lattice is one of the simplest objects that combines continuous geometry with discrete arithmetic.

Start with linearly independent vectors:

$$
b_1,\ldots,b_n\in\mathbb R^m.
$$

Their real span is continuous:

$$
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}(b_1,\ldots,b_n).
$$

Their integer span is not:

$$
\boxed{
L
=
\left\{
\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_i b_i:
z_i\in\mathbb Z
\right\}.
}
$$

This discrete set is a **lattice**.

The distinction is small syntactically:

$$
\mathbb R
\longrightarrow
\mathbb Z,
$$

but enormous mathematically.

Lattice theory studies the interaction between:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Euclidean geometry}
}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{
\text{integer structure}.
}
$$

This article develops the basic objects that will remain invariant throughout the rest of the series:

$$
\text{rank},
\qquad
\text{determinant},
\qquad
\text{successive minima},
\qquad
\text{dual lattice}.
$$

The basis used to describe a lattice may change dramatically.

The lattice itself does not.

## What is a lattice?

Let:

$$
b_1,\ldots,b_n
\in
\mathbb R^m
$$

be linearly independent.

Place them as columns of the matrix:

$$
\boxed{
B=
\begin{pmatrix}
|&&|\\
b_1&\cdots&b_n\\
|&&|
\end{pmatrix}
\in
\mathbb R^{m\times n}.
}
$$

Because the columns are linearly independent:

$$
\operatorname{rank}(B)=n.
$$

The lattice generated by $B$ is:

$$
\boxed{
L(B)
=
B\mathbb Z^n
=
\{
Bz:z\in\mathbb Z^n
\}.
}
$$

Equivalently:

$$
\boxed{
L(B)
=
\left\{
\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_i b_i:
z_i\in\mathbb Z
\right\}.
}
$$

---

### Rank and ambient dimension

The number:

$$
\boxed{
n
}
$$

is the **rank** of the lattice.

The space containing the lattice is:

$$
\mathbb R^m.
$$

Thus:

$$
n\le m.
$$

If:

$$
n=m,
$$

the lattice is called **full-rank** in:

$$
\mathbb R^n.
$$

If:

$$
n<m,
$$

the lattice occupies only the $n$-dimensional real subspace:

$$
\boxed{
V_L
=
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}(L)
=
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}(b_1,\ldots,b_n).
}
$$

---

### Example

Let:

$$
b_1=
\begin{pmatrix}
2\\
0
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
b_2=
\begin{pmatrix}
1\\
3
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Then:

$$
B=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&1\\
0&3
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The generated lattice is:

$$
L(B)
=
\left\{
z_1
\begin{pmatrix}
2\\
0
\end{pmatrix}
+
z_2
\begin{pmatrix}
1\\
3
\end{pmatrix}
:
z_1,z_2\in\mathbb Z
\right\}.
$$

Equivalently:

$$
\boxed{
L(B)
=
\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}
2z_1+z_2\\
3z_2
\end{pmatrix}
:
z_1,z_2\in\mathbb Z
\right\}.
}
$$

The real span is:

$$
\mathbb R^2.
$$

But the lattice contains only a discrete collection of points inside that continuous plane.

---

### Why lattices are discrete

Because the generating vectors are linearly independent, the map:

$$
B:\mathbb R^n\rightarrow V_L
$$

is injective.

The coefficient set:

$$
\mathbb Z^n
$$

is discrete.

Therefore:

$$
B\mathbb Z^n
$$

is also discrete inside its span.

Informally, every lattice point has some sufficiently small neighborhood containing no other lattice point.

This discreteness is what allows quantities such as:

$$
\boxed{
\text{the shortest nonzero vector}
}
$$

to make sense.

A continuous vector space would contain arbitrarily small nonzero scalar multiples of every vector.

A lattice does not.

---

### Equivalent abstract definition

A lattice can also be defined as a **discrete additive subgroup** of:

$$
\mathbb R^m.
$$

A fundamental structural theorem says that every such subgroup has the form:

$$
\boxed{
\mathbb Zb_1+\cdots+\mathbb Zb_n
}
$$

for linearly independent real vectors:

$$
b_1,\ldots,b_n.
$$

Thus the two viewpoints agree:

$$
\boxed{
\text{discrete subgroup}
\iff
\text{integer span of independent vectors}.
}
$$

---

### Integer-coordinate lattices

If:

$$
B\in\mathbb Z^{m\times n},
$$

then:

$$
L(B)\subseteq\mathbb Z^m.
$$

This is often called an **integer lattice** in computational settings.

It is useful to distinguish this phrase from the algebraic notion of an **integral lattice**, where one instead requires:

$$
\langle x,y\rangle\in\mathbb Z
$$

for all lattice vectors:

$$
x,y\in L.
$$

The two notions are related but not identical.

In this series, the intended meaning will always be clear from context.

---

## A basis is not the lattice

One of the most important ideas in lattice theory is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{the basis is a description of the lattice, not the lattice itself}.
}
$$

A single lattice usually has infinitely many bases.

Some are short and nearly orthogonal.

Others are long and extremely skewed.

Yet they generate exactly the same set of lattice points.

---

### Unimodular matrices

An integer matrix:

$$
U\in\mathbb Z^{n\times n}
$$

is **unimodular** if:

$$
\boxed{
\det U=\pm1.
}
$$

Equivalently:

$$
\boxed{
U\in GL_n(\mathbb Z).
}
$$

The inverse is again integral:

$$
U^{-1}\in\mathbb Z^{n\times n}.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
U\mathbb Z^n
=
\mathbb Z^n.
}
$$

---

### Unimodular basis changes preserve the lattice

Let:

$$
B'=BU
$$

with:

$$
U\in GL_n(\mathbb Z).
$$

Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
L(B')
&=
BU\mathbb Z^n
\\
&=
B\mathbb Z^n
\\
&=
L(B).
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
L(BU)=L(B).
}
$$

---

### The converse

Suppose:

$$
B,C\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}
$$

both have full column rank and generate the same rank-$n$ lattice:

$$
L(B)=L(C).
$$

Because every column of $C$ belongs to $L(B)$, there exists:

$$
U\in\mathbb Z^{n\times n}
$$

such that:

$$
C=BU.
$$

Likewise, because every column of $B$ belongs to $L(C)$, there exists:

$$
V\in\mathbb Z^{n\times n}
$$

such that:

$$
B=CV.
$$

Hence:

$$
B=BUV.
$$

Since $B$ has full column rank:

$$
UV=I.
$$

Thus:

$$
U^{-1}=V
$$

is integral.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
U\in GL_n(\mathbb Z).
}
$$

So we obtain the exact characterization:

$$
\boxed{
L(B)=L(C)
\iff
C=BU
\text{ for some }
U\in GL_n(\mathbb Z).
}
$$

---

### Example of a valid basis change

Take:

$$
B=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&1\\
0&3
\end{pmatrix}
$$

and:

$$
U=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&-1\\
0&1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Since:

$$
\det U=1,
$$

the matrix is unimodular.

Then:

$$
BU
=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&-1\\
0&3
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

So the new basis vectors are:

$$
b_1'=
b_1,
$$

$$
b_2'
=
b_2-b_1.
$$

The basis changed, but:

$$
\boxed{
L(BU)=L(B).
}
$$

---

### Same real span does not imply same lattice

Now take:

$$
M=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&0\\
0&1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

This is invertible over:

$$
\mathbb R,
$$

so:

$$
BM
$$

has the same real span as $B$.

But:

$$
\det M=2.
$$

Therefore:

$$
M\notin GL_2(\mathbb Z).
$$

The lattice generated by $BM$ is a proper sublattice:

$$
\boxed{
L(BM)\subsetneq L(B).
}
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}(B)
=
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}(BM)
}
$$

does **not** imply:

$$
\boxed{
L(B)=L(BM).
}
$$

This is the central distinction between ordinary linear algebra and lattice theory.

---

## Fundamental parallelepipeds and quotient geometry

A basis:

$$
B=(b_1,\ldots,b_n)
$$

defines the half-open **fundamental parallelepiped**:

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal P(B)
=
\left\{
Bt:
t\in[0,1)^n
\right\}.
}
$$

Equivalently:

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal P(B)
=
\left\{
\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_i b_i:
0\le t_i<1
\right\}.
}
$$

---

### Every point has a unique representative

Take:

$$
x\in V_L.
$$

Because $B$ is a basis of:

$$
V_L,
$$

there is a unique coefficient vector:

$$
c\in\mathbb R^n
$$

such that:

$$
x=Bc.
$$

Write each coordinate as:

$$
c_i=z_i+t_i,
$$

where:

$$
z_i=\lfloor c_i\rfloor\in\mathbb Z
$$

and:

$$
0\le t_i<1.
$$

Then:

$$
c=z+t
$$

with:

$$
z\in\mathbb Z^n,
\qquad
t\in[0,1)^n.
$$

Therefore:

$$
x=Bz+Bt.
$$

The term:

$$
Bz
$$

belongs to the lattice, while:

$$
Bt\in\mathcal P(B).
$$

Hence:

$$
\boxed{
x
=
v+p,
\qquad
v\in L,
\quad
p\in\mathcal P(B).
}
$$

Because the region is half-open, the representative $p$ is unique.

---

### Tiling the span

The translates:

$$
\mathcal P(B)+v,
\qquad
v\in L,
$$

tile:

$$
V_L
$$

without overlapping in their interiors.

Symbolically:

$$
\boxed{
V_L
=
\bigsqcup_{v\in L}
\left(
\mathcal P(B)+v
\right)
}
$$

up to boundary conventions.

So the fundamental parallelepiped is one geometric cell whose lattice translates reproduce the entire span.

---

### Quotient interpretation

Points differing by a lattice vector are equivalent:

$$
x\sim y
\iff
x-y\in L.
$$

The quotient:

$$
\boxed{
V_L/L
}
$$

therefore identifies all lattice translates.

The fundamental parallelepiped gives one representative for every equivalence class.

In the full-rank case:

$$
L\subset\mathbb R^n,
$$

the quotient:

$$
\mathbb R^n/L
$$

is an $n$-dimensional real torus.

This viewpoint becomes important in:

- Fourier analysis;
- periodic functions;
- dual lattices;
- Gaussian distributions over lattices.

---

## Determinant, covolume, and sublattice index

The geometric size of a fundamental cell is an invariant of the lattice.

It is called the **determinant** or **covolume** of the lattice.

---

### Full-rank square basis

If:

$$
B\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}
$$

is a full-rank basis matrix, then:

$$
\boxed{
\det(L)
=
|\det B|.
}
$$

This is the volume of:

$$
\mathcal P(B).
$$

The absolute value appears because geometric volume does not depend on orientation.

---

### Rectangular basis

Suppose instead:

$$
B\in\mathbb R^{m\times n},
\qquad
n\le m,
$$

with full column rank.

Then $B$ has no ordinary square determinant.

Its intrinsic $n$-dimensional volume is:

$$
\boxed{
\det(L)
=
\sqrt{
\det(B^TB)
}.
}
$$

The matrix:

$$
G=B^TB
$$

is the Gram matrix.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
\det(L)
=
\sqrt{\det G}.
}
$$

---

### Connection with Gram–Schmidt

Let:

$$
b_1^*,\ldots,b_n^*
$$

be the Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization of the basis.

From the previous Linear Algebra Foundations article:

$$
\boxed{
\det(L)
=
\prod_{i=1}^{n}
\|b_i^*\|_2.
}
$$

Thus the same invariant appears in three forms:

$$
\boxed{
|\det B|
}
$$

for a square basis,

$$
\boxed{
\sqrt{\det(B^TB)}
}
$$

for a general full-column-rank basis, and:

$$
\boxed{
\prod_i\|b_i^*\|.
}
$$

---

### Example

Take again:

$$
B=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&1\\
0&3
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Then:

$$
\det B=6.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\det(L)=6.
}
$$

The fundamental parallelogram has Euclidean area:

$$
6.
$$

---

### Basis invariance

Suppose:

$$
B'=BU
$$

with:

$$
U\in GL_n(\mathbb Z).
$$

In the square case:

$$
\det(B')
=
\det(B)\det(U).
$$

Since:

$$
|\det U|=1,
$$

we obtain:

$$
\boxed{
|\det(B')|
=
|\det(B)|.
}
$$

Thus the covolume depends on the lattice, not on the chosen basis.

---

### Density interpretation

For lattices of the same rank in the same Euclidean scale, smaller determinant means a smaller fundamental cell.

Informally, this means the lattice has more points per unit volume in its span.

The asymptotic point density is:

$$
\boxed{
\frac{1}{\det(L)}.
}
$$

More precisely, for large sufficiently regular regions $S$,

$$
|L\cap S|
$$

is heuristically and asymptotically related to:

$$
\frac{
\operatorname{vol}(S)
}{
\det(L)
}.
$$

This volume-per-point principle lies behind much of the geometry of numbers.

---

### Sublattices

A lattice:

$$
L'\subseteq L
$$

of the same rank is called a **full-rank sublattice** of $L$.

The quotient group:

$$
L/L'
$$

is finite.

Its cardinality is the **index**:

$$
\boxed{
[L:L'].
}
$$

---

### Determinant and index

Suppose:

$$
L=L(B)
$$

and:

$$
L'=L(BM)
$$

for an integer matrix:

$$
M\in\mathbb Z^{n\times n}
$$

with:

$$
\det M\neq0.
$$

Then:

$$
L'\subseteq L.
$$

The index is:

$$
\boxed{
[L:L']
=
|\det M|.
}
$$

Consequently:

$$
\boxed{
\det(L')
=
[L:L']
\det(L).
}
$$

This equation generalizes the unimodular case.

If:

$$
|\det M|=1,
$$

the index is $1$, so:

$$
L'=L.
$$

If:

$$
|\det M|>1,
$$

the new lattice is a proper sublattice and its fundamental cell is correspondingly larger.

---

### Example

Let:

$$
L=\mathbb Z^2.
$$

Take:

$$
M=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&0\\
0&1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Then:

$$
L'
=
2\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z.
$$

We have:

$$
|\det M|=2.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
[\mathbb Z^2:L']=2.
}
$$

Also:

$$
\det(\mathbb Z^2)=1,
$$

while:

$$
\det(L')=2.
$$

Indeed:

$$
\boxed{
\det(L')
=
2\det(\mathbb Z^2).
}
$$

## Shortest vectors and successive minima

The determinant measures global density.

It does not tell us exactly where the shortest vectors are.

For that we introduce the **successive minima**.

---

### The first minimum

Let:

$$
L
$$

be a lattice.

Because $L$ is discrete, there is a positive minimum distance from the origin to any nonzero lattice point.

Define:

$$
\boxed{
\lambda_1(L)
=
\min_{
v\in L\setminus\{0\}
}
\|v\|_2.
}
$$

This is the length of a shortest nonzero lattice vector.

Any vector:

$$
v\in L
$$

satisfying:

$$
\|v\|_2
=
\lambda_1(L)
$$

is called a **shortest vector**.

There may be several shortest vectors.

---

### Successive minima

Let:

$$
B_2^m
=
\{
x\in\mathbb R^m:
\|x\|_2\le1
\}
$$

denote the Euclidean unit ball.

For a rank-$n$ lattice, define:

$$
\boxed{
\lambda_i(L)
=
\inf
\left\{
r>0:
\dim
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}
\left(
L\cap rB_2^m
\right)
\ge i
\right\}.
}
$$

Because lattices are discrete, these infima are attained.

Thus:

$$
\lambda_i(L)
$$

is the smallest radius containing $i$ linearly independent lattice vectors.

---

### The sequence

By definition:

$$
\boxed{
\lambda_1(L)
\le
\lambda_2(L)
\le
\cdots
\le
\lambda_n(L).
}
$$

Here:

$$
\lambda_1(L)
$$

measures the shortest nonzero vector.

Meanwhile:

$$
\lambda_n(L)
$$

is the smallest radius containing enough linearly independent lattice vectors to span:

$$
V_L.
$$

---

### Important subtlety

Vectors achieving:

$$
\lambda_1(L),\ldots,\lambda_n(L)
$$

need not form a basis of the entire lattice.

They may generate a proper finite-index sublattice.

This is an important distinction:

$$
\boxed{
\text{linearly independent short vectors}
\neq
\text{necessarily a lattice basis}.
}
$$

A lattice basis must generate **every** lattice vector using integer coefficients.

---

### Basis independence

The quantities:

$$
\lambda_i(L)
$$

depend only on the set of lattice points.

Therefore they are unchanged under:

$$
B
\longrightarrow
BU,
\qquad
U\in GL_n(\mathbb Z).
$$

So:

$$
\boxed{
\lambda_i(L)
}
$$

is a lattice invariant.

A poor basis may contain extremely long vectors even when:

$$
\lambda_1(L)
$$

is small.

That is precisely why basis reduction is useful.

---

### Basis length does not equal shortest-vector length

Suppose:

$$
B=(b_1,\ldots,b_n).
$$

Certainly:

$$
b_i\in L.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\lambda_1(L)
\le
\min_i\|b_i\|.
$$

But equality need not hold.

A shorter lattice vector may arise as an integer combination such as:

$$
b_2-17b_1
$$

or a much more complicated combination.

So the basis can hide the actual geometry of the lattice.

This observation leads directly toward the Shortest Vector Problem.

## Dual lattices

Duality introduces another lattice whose vectors record all integer-valued inner products with the original lattice.

This will become fundamental in:

- Fourier analysis;
- Poisson summation;
- Gaussian measures;
- transference theorems;
- SIS and LWE analysis;
- dual lattice attacks.

---

### Full-rank definition

Let:

$$
L\subseteq\mathbb R^n
$$

be a full-rank lattice.

Its **dual lattice** is:

$$
\boxed{
L^*
=
\{
y\in\mathbb R^n:
\langle x,y\rangle\in\mathbb Z
\text{ for every }x\in L
\}.
}
$$

It is enough to test the basis vectors.

If:

$$
L=L(B),
$$

then:

$$
y\in L^*
$$

exactly when:

$$
\boxed{
B^Ty\in\mathbb Z^n.
}
$$

---

### Dual basis

Suppose:

$$
B\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}
$$

is invertible.

Define:

$$
\boxed{
B^*
=
B^{-T}.
}
$$

Then:

$$
B^TB^*
=
I_n.
$$

So if:

$$
b_i
$$

are the columns of $B$ and:

$$
b_j^*
$$

are the columns of $B^*$, then:

$$
\boxed{
\langle b_i,b_j^*\rangle
=
\delta_{ij}.
}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
L^*
=
L(B^{-T}).
}
$$

---

### Important notation warning

The notation:

$$
b_i^*
$$

is often also used for Gram–Schmidt vectors.

These are completely different objects.

A **Gram–Schmidt vector** is an orthogonal geometric auxiliary vector.

A **dual basis vector** satisfies:

$$
\langle b_i,b_j^\vee\rangle
=
\delta_{ij}.
$$

To avoid confusion, one may denote the dual basis by:

$$
B^\vee
$$

rather than:

$$
B^*.
$$

In this article:

$$
L^*
$$

always denotes the dual lattice, while the surrounding text will make clear whether a starred vector belongs to Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization or to the dual basis.

---

### Example

Take:

$$
B=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&1\\
0&3
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its inverse is:

$$
B^{-1}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
1/2&-1/6\\
0&1/3
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Therefore:

$$
B^{-T}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
1/2&0\\
-1/6&1/3
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The dual basis vectors are:

$$
d_1=
\begin{pmatrix}
1/2\\
-1/6
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
d_2=
\begin{pmatrix}
0\\
1/3
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Check:

$$
\langle b_1,d_1\rangle
=
1,
$$

$$
\langle b_1,d_2\rangle
=
0,
$$

$$
\langle b_2,d_1\rangle
=
0,
$$

$$
\langle b_2,d_2\rangle
=
1.
$$

So:

$$
B^TB^{-T}=I.
$$

---

### Dual determinant

For a full-rank lattice:

$$
\det(L)
=
|\det B|.
$$

The dual basis is:

$$
B^{-T}.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det(L^*)
&=
|\det(B^{-T})|
\\
&=
\frac{1}{|\det B|}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence:

$$
\boxed{
\det(L^*)
=
\frac{1}{\det(L)}.
}
$$

So a sparse lattice has a correspondingly dense dual, and vice versa.

---

### Double dual

Applying duality twice returns the original lattice:

$$
\boxed{
(L^*)^*=L.
}
$$

At the basis level:

$$
(B^{-T})^{-T}=B.
$$

This is one indication that duality is a genuine symmetry rather than merely an auxiliary construction.

---

### Non-full-rank lattices

Suppose now:

$$
L\subset\mathbb R^m
$$

has rank:

$$
n<m.
$$

A naive definition:

$$
\{
y\in\mathbb R^m:
\langle x,y\rangle\in\mathbb Z
\text{ for all }x\in L
\}
$$

contains the entire orthogonal complement:

$$
V_L^\perp.
$$

That set is therefore not discrete.

So for a non-full-rank lattice, the natural **intrinsic dual** is defined inside its real span:

$$
\boxed{
L^*
=
\{
y\in V_L:
\langle x,y\rangle\in\mathbb Z
\text{ for every }x\in L
\}.
}
$$

---

### Rectangular dual basis

Let:

$$
B\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}
$$

have full column rank.

We seek a matrix:

$$
D\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}
$$

whose columns lie in:

$$
V_L
$$

and satisfy:

$$
\boxed{
B^TD=I_n.
}
$$

Since:

$$
D
=
B(B^TB)^{-1}
$$

gives:

$$
B^TD
=
B^TB(B^TB)^{-1}
=
I_n,
$$

an intrinsic dual basis is:

$$
\boxed{
B^\vee
=
B(B^TB)^{-1}.
}
$$

When $B$ is square:

$$
B(B^TB)^{-1}
=
B^{-T},
$$

so this reduces to the familiar formula.

---

### Covolume of the intrinsic dual

Using:

$$
B^\vee
=
B(B^TB)^{-1},
$$

we obtain:

$$
(B^\vee)^TB^\vee
=
(B^TB)^{-1}.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\det(L^*)
=
\sqrt{
\det(
(B^TB)^{-1}
)
}.
$$

Hence:

$$
\boxed{
\det(L^*)
=
\frac{1}{\det(L)}
}
$$

when both determinants are measured intrinsically inside the common $n$-dimensional span.

---

## The computational viewpoint

The definitions above already contain the basic computational questions of lattice theory.

Given:

$$
B,
$$

we can easily produce lattice vectors:

$$
v=Bz,
\qquad
z\in\mathbb Z^n.
$$

The difficult direction is often the reverse.

Given geometric information, can we recover useful integer coefficients?

---

### Shortest Vector Problem

The **Shortest Vector Problem (SVP)** asks for:

$$
\boxed{
v\in L\setminus\{0\}
}
$$

such that:

$$
\boxed{
\|v\|_2
=
\lambda_1(L).
}
$$

This is an intrinsic property of the lattice.

It does not ask for the shortest basis vector.

---

### Closest Vector Problem

Given a target:

$$
t\in V_L,
$$

the **Closest Vector Problem (CVP)** asks for:

$$
\boxed{
v\in L
}
$$

minimizing:

$$
\boxed{
\|t-v\|_2.
}
$$

Equivalently:

$$
\boxed{
\operatorname{dist}(t,L)
=
\min_{v\in L}
\|t-v\|_2.
}
$$

SVP studies short vectors relative to the origin.

CVP introduces an arbitrary external target.

---

### The basis-quality problem

Two matrices:

$$
B
$$

and:

$$
BU
$$

may generate exactly the same lattice while having dramatically different geometry.

One basis might look like:

$$
\boxed{
\text{short and nearly orthogonal}.
}
$$

Another may be:

$$
\boxed{
\text{long and highly skewed}.
}
$$

The computational problems belong to the lattice.

But the difficulty of many practical algorithms depends strongly on the quality of the available basis.

This leads to **basis reduction**.

---

### What lattice reduction tries to do

A reduction algorithm seeks a new basis:

$$
\boxed{
B'=BU,
\qquad
U\in GL_n(\mathbb Z),
}
$$

such that:

$$
L(B')=L(B),
$$

while the new basis has better geometric properties.

The lattice determinant stays fixed:

$$
\boxed{
\det(L(B'))
=
\det(L(B)).
}
$$

But quantities such as:

- basis-vector lengths;
- angles;
- Gram–Schmidt coefficients;
- Gram–Schmidt length profile;

can improve dramatically.

---

### LLL does not solve exact SVP

The Lenstra–Lenstra–Lovász algorithm, or **LLL**, is a polynomial-time basis-reduction algorithm.

It does **not** provide a polynomial-time algorithm for exact Euclidean SVP.

Instead, LLL efficiently produces a basis satisfying quantitative reduction conditions and gives approximation guarantees.

In practice, it often reveals useful short vectors.

But:

$$
\boxed{
\text{reduced basis}
\neq
\text{exact shortest-vector oracle}.
}
$$

This distinction will remain important throughout the series.

---

### A small exact sanity check

For a $2\times2$ integer basis, basic invariants can be verified with a few lines of Python:

```python
from math import gcd


def det2(M):
    return (
        M[0][0] * M[1][1]
        - M[0][1] * M[1][0]
    )


def is_unimodular_2x2(U):
    return abs(det2(U)) == 1


B = [
    [2, 1],
    [0, 3],
]

U = [
    [1, -1],
    [0, 1],
]

print(det2(B))
print(det2(U))
print(is_unimodular_2x2(U))
```

Expected output:

```text
6
1
True
```

The first number is the lattice covolume:

$$
\det(L)=6.
$$

The second confirms that the basis transformation is unimodular.

---

### What should be tested in implementations

Whenever lattice software changes a basis, useful invariants include:

$$
\boxed{
\det(L)
}
$$

and, where an explicit transformation matrix is available:

$$
\boxed{
B'=BU,
\qquad
U\in GL_n(\mathbb Z).
}
$$

For integer square bases, one can verify:

$$
|\det B'|
=
|\det B|.
$$

For rectangular bases:

$$
\det(B'^TB')
=
\det(B^TB).
$$

These checks do not prove that a reduction algorithm is correct in every respect, but they catch many accidental basis transformations that actually change the lattice.

## A complete worked example

Consider:

$$
B=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&1\\
0&3
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its columns are:

$$
b_1=
\begin{pmatrix}
2\\
0
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
b_2=
\begin{pmatrix}
1\\
3
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

---

### Step 1 — The lattice

Every lattice vector has the form:

$$
v
=
z_1b_1+z_2b_2.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
v
=
\begin{pmatrix}
2z_1+z_2\\
3z_2
\end{pmatrix}.
}
$$

---

### Step 2 — The determinant

We compute:

$$
\det B
=
2\cdot3-0\cdot1
=
6.
$$

So:

$$
\boxed{
\det(L)=6.
}
$$

---

### Step 3 — A new basis

Take:

$$
U=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&-1\\
0&1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Since:

$$
\det U=1,
$$

the matrix is unimodular.

Then:

$$
B'=BU
=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&-1\\
0&3
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Hence:

$$
\boxed{
L(B')=L(B).
}
$$

---

### Step 4 — A transformation that changes the lattice

Take instead:

$$
M=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&0\\
0&1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Now:

$$
\det M=2.
$$

Therefore:

$$
L(BM)
$$

is a sublattice of index:

$$
2.
$$

Its determinant is:

$$
\boxed{
\det(L(BM))
=
2\cdot6
=
12.
}
$$

So the real span did not change, but the lattice did.

---

### Step 5 — The dual

We have:

$$
B^{-T}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
1/2&0\\
-1/6&1/3
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
L^*
=
L(B^{-T}).
}
$$

Its determinant is:

$$
\boxed{
\det(L^*)
=
\frac16.
}
$$

And indeed:

$$
\boxed{
\det(L)\det(L^*)=1.
}
$$

## The structural picture

A lattice begins with:

$$
\boxed{
L(B)=B\mathbb Z^n.
}
$$

Its real span is:

$$
\boxed{
V_L=B\mathbb R^n.
}
$$

The lattice is discrete because the coefficients are restricted to:

$$
\mathbb Z^n.
$$

Different bases describe the same lattice exactly when:

$$
\boxed{
B'
=
BU,
\qquad
U\in GL_n(\mathbb Z).
}
$$

Each basis defines a fundamental cell:

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal P(B)
=
B[0,1)^n.
}
$$

Its intrinsic volume is:

$$
\boxed{
\det(L)
=
\sqrt{\det(B^TB)}.
}
$$

Short-vector geometry is captured by:

$$
\boxed{
\lambda_1(L)
\le
\cdots
\le
\lambda_n(L).
}
$$

The dual lattice records integer-valued inner products:

$$
\boxed{
L^*
=
\{
y:
\langle x,y\rangle\in\mathbb Z
\text{ for every }x\in L
\}.
}
$$

For full-rank square $B$:

$$
\boxed{
L^*=L(B^{-T}).
}
$$

And:

$$
\boxed{
\det(L^*)
=
\frac1{\det(L)}.
}
$$

So the foundational invariant picture is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{basis}
\longrightarrow
\text{lattice}
\longrightarrow
\text{fundamental cell}
\longrightarrow
\det(L)
}
$$

together with:

$$
\boxed{
\text{short-vector geometry}
\longrightarrow
\lambda_i(L)
}
$$

and:

$$
\boxed{
\text{inner-product arithmetic}
\longrightarrow
L^*.
}
$$
