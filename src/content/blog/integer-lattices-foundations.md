A lattice is a discrete additive subgroup of Euclidean space. Equivalently, it is the set of all integer combinations of linearly independent basis vectors.

## 1. Definition

Let $b_1,\dots,b_n\in\mathbb R^m$ be linearly independent. The lattice generated by them is
$$
L(B)=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^n z_i b_i:z_i\in\mathbb Z\right\}.
$$

The rank is $n$; the ambient dimension is $m$.

## 2. Lattice bases are not unique

If $U\in GL_n(\mathbb Z)$ is unimodular, then
$$
L(BU)=L(B).
$$

Conversely, two full-rank bases generate the same lattice exactly when they differ by a unimodular transformation.

This is the key distinction from arbitrary real basis changes: a non-integral change of basis may preserve the real span while changing the lattice.

## 3. Fundamental parallelepiped

A basis defines
$$
\mathcal P(B)=\left\{B t:t\in[0,1)^n\right\}.
$$
Every point in the real span is congruent modulo the lattice to exactly one point of this half-open region.

The volume of $\mathcal P(B)$ is the lattice determinant.

## 4. Lattice determinant

For a square full-rank basis matrix,
$$
\det L=|\det B|.
$$
For a rank-$n$ lattice in $\mathbb R^m$,
$$
\det L=\sqrt{\det(B^TB)}.
$$

It measures density: smaller determinant means lattice points are packed more densely in their span.

## 5. Shortest vectors and successive minima

The shortest-vector length is
$$
\lambda_1(L)=\min_{0\ne v\in L}\|v\|.
$$
More generally, $\lambda_i(L)$ is the smallest radius containing $i$ linearly independent lattice vectors.

These geometric invariants are basis-independent, even though a bad basis can hide the short vectors.

## 6. Dual lattice

For a full-rank lattice $L\subset\mathbb R^n$,
$$
L^*=\{y\in\mathbb R^n:\langle x,y\rangle\in\mathbb Z\text{ for all }x\in L\}.
$$
If $B$ is a basis matrix with column convention, then a dual basis is
$$
B^*=B^{-T}.
$$

One has
$$
\det L^*=\frac1{\det L}.
$$

Duality is fundamental in Fourier analysis on lattices and modern lattice cryptography.

## 7. Computational problems

Two canonical problems are:

- **SVP:** find a shortest nonzero vector;
- **CVP:** find a lattice point closest to a target.

LLL does not solve exact SVP in polynomial time. Instead, it efficiently finds a basis with provably controlled quality and often exposes useful short vectors.
