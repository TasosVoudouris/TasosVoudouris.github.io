Vector spaces are defined over fields. Many mathematical objects used in cryptography instead carry scalars from rings such as $\mathbb Z$ or $\mathbb Z_q[x]$. The correct generalization is a **module**.

## 1. Definition

Let $R$ be a ring. An $R$-module $M$ is an abelian group under addition together with scalar multiplication
$$
R\times M\to M,
\qquad
(r,m)\mapsto rm,
$$
satisfying the familiar distributive and associative laws.

The key difference from a vector space is that nonzero scalars need not be invertible.

## 2. Examples

- Every vector space over a field $F$ is an $F$-module.
- Every abelian group is naturally a $\mathbb Z$-module via repeated addition.
- $\mathbb Z^n$ is a free $\mathbb Z$-module.
- $R[x]^m$ is an $R[x]$-module.
- Ideals of a commutative ring are submodules of the ring viewed as a module over itself.

## 3. Submodules

A subset $N\subseteq M$ is a submodule if it is closed under addition, additive inverses, and multiplication by all scalars in $R$.

As with groups, one can form quotient modules $M/N$.

## 4. Module homomorphisms

A map $f:M\to N$ between $R$-modules is $R$-linear if
$$
f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y),
\qquad
f(rx)=rf(x).
$$

Kernels, images, quotients, and isomorphism theorems extend naturally from groups and rings to modules.

## 5. Free modules and bases

A module is **free** if it has a basis in the same formal sense as a vector space: every element has a unique finite linear combination in terms of basis vectors.

But unlike vector spaces, not every module is free and bases need not behave as uniformly across arbitrary rings.

For the PID $\mathbb Z$, finitely generated modules have the structure described by the structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups.

## 6. Why lattices are modules

A full-rank integer lattice in $\mathbb R^n$ generated by linearly independent vectors $b_1,\dots,b_n$ is
$$
L=\left\{z_1b_1+\cdots+z_nb_n:z_i\in\mathbb Z\right\}.
$$
So $L$ is a free $\mathbb Z$-module embedded in a real vector space.

This is precisely why lattice theory sits between abstract algebra and Euclidean geometry: the coefficients are discrete integers, while lengths and angles live in $\mathbb R^n$.
