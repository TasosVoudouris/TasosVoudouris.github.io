FROST is easier to understand after deriving the ordinary Schnorr equation and
then replacing its secret-key term with Shamir-weighted key shares.

## Learning goals

This chapter develops:

- ordinary Schnorr signing and verification;
- Lagrange interpolation at zero;
- the two-nonce FROST response equation;
- individual signature-share verification;
- the aggregate verification proof; and
- one complete worked example from the Version 0.5 code.

## Notation

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $q$ | Prime scalar-field order |
| $G=\langle g\rangle$ | Prime-order group generated by $g$ |
| $x$ | Group secret signing scalar |
| $Y=g^x$ | Group public key |
| $x_i=f(i)$ | Participant $i$'s Shamir key share |
| $Y_i=g^{x_i}$ | Participant $i$'s public verification share |
| $S$ | Selected signer-identifier set |
| $m$ | Exact byte string passed to the FROST core |
| $D_i,E_i$ | Hiding and binding nonce commitments |
| $\rho_i$ | Binding factor for signer $i$ |
| $\lambda_i$ | Lagrange coefficient for signer $i$ at zero |
| $c$ | Schnorr challenge |
| $z_i$ | Participant signature share |
| $(R,z)$ | Final Schnorr signature |

RFC 9591 uses additive notation for elliptic-curve groups. CryptoCave's prior
modules use a multiplicative subgroup, so this text writes multiplication and
exponentiation. The equations are equivalent under the mapping

$$
A+B\longleftrightarrow A\cdot B,
\qquad
[s]A\longleftrightarrow A^s.
$$

## Ordinary Schnorr signatures

The signer owns $x\in\mathbb{F}_q$ and publishes $Y=g^x$.

To sign $m$:

1. sample a fresh nonce $r\in\mathbb{F}_q$;
2. compute $R=g^r$;
3. compute $c=H_2(\operatorname{enc}(R)\|\operatorname{enc}(Y)\|m)$;
4. compute $z=r+cx\pmod q$; and
5. output $(R,z)$.

The verifier checks

$$
g^z\stackrel{?}{=}R\,Y^c.
$$

Correctness follows immediately:

$$
g^z
=g^{r+cx}
=g^r(g^x)^c
=R Y^c.
$$

The final Version 0.5 signature uses this exact verification shape. Function
`verify_schnorr_signature()` knows nothing about Shamir shares or a threshold
protocol.

## Interpolating the secret in the exponent

Let $S$ be any set of at least $t$ distinct nonzero participant identifiers.
For $i\in S$, define

$$
\lambda_i^S
=\prod_{j\in S,\,j\ne i}\frac{j}{j-i}\pmod q.
$$

This is the Lagrange basis polynomial for point $i$, evaluated at zero. For a
degree-$(t-1)$ DKG polynomial,

$$
x=f(0)=\sum_{i\in S}\lambda_i^S f(i)
=\sum_{i\in S}\lambda_i^S x_i\pmod q.
$$

No participant needs to evaluate that full sum. Each signer uses only its own
term $\lambda_i^Sx_i$ inside $z_i$.

### Identifier rules are security-relevant

The denominator contains $j-i$, so identifiers must be distinct. Identifier
zero is forbidden because $f(0)$ is the secret itself. RFC 9591 represents
identifiers as distinct `NonZeroScalar` values. Version 0.5 enforces the same
algebraic rule.

## Why FROST uses two nonces

Signer $i$ samples two one-time secret scalars:

$$
d_i\quad\text{(hiding nonce)},
\qquad
e_i\quad\text{(binding nonce)}.
$$

It publishes

$$
D_i=g^{d_i},\qquad E_i=g^{e_i}.
$$

After the coordinator fixes the message and complete commitment list, everyone
derives a signer-specific factor $\rho_i$. The effective commitment of signer
$i$ is

$$
R_i=D_iE_i^{\rho_i}=g^{d_i+e_i\rho_i}.
$$

The group commitment is

$$
R=\prod_{i\in S}R_i
=\prod_{i\in S}D_iE_i^{\rho_i}.
$$

The binding factor makes a commitment's contribution depend on the group key,
message, complete sorted commitment list, and signer identifier. This prevents
the commitment-manipulation attack that affected simpler threshold Schnorr
constructions.

## Signature shares

All signers compute the same $R$ and challenge

$$
c=H_2(\operatorname{enc}(R)\|\operatorname{enc}(Y)\|m).
$$

Signer $i$ returns

$$
z_i=d_i+e_i\rho_i+\lambda_i^Sx_i c\pmod q.
$$

The coordinator can check the response without learning $d_i$, $e_i$, or
$x_i$:

$$
g^{z_i}
\stackrel{?}{=}
D_iE_i^{\rho_i}Y_i^{c\lambda_i^S}.
$$

The right side is public because $D_i$, $E_i$, $Y_i$, $c$, $\rho_i$, and
$\lambda_i^S$ are public.

## Aggregation proof

The coordinator computes

$$
z=\sum_{i\in S}z_i\pmod q.
$$

Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
g^z
&=\prod_{i\in S}g^{z_i}\\
&=\prod_{i\in S}
  D_iE_i^{\rho_i}Y_i^{c\lambda_i^S}\\
&=R\prod_{i\in S}(g^{x_i})^{c\lambda_i^S}\\
&=R\,g^{c\sum_{i\in S}\lambda_i^Sx_i}\\
&=R\,g^{cx}\\
&=R\,Y^c.
\end{aligned}
$$

That is the ordinary Schnorr verification equation. Threshold participation is
visible during production, but not required by the final algebraic verifier.

## Complete Version 0.5 worked example

The deterministic `examples/frost_demo.py` run uses the order-41 subgroup of
$\mathbb{Z}_{83}^*$ with generator $g=4$. Its DKG produces:

$$
q=41,\qquad p=83,\qquad Y=48.
$$

The selected signer set is

$$
S=\{1,3,5\}.
$$

The public verification shares are

$$
Y_1=49,\qquad Y_3=59,\qquad Y_5=3.
$$

Round one publishes:

| $i$ | $D_i$ | $E_i$ |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 31 | 69 |
| 3 | 25 | 12 |
| 5 | 23 | 59 |

After binding the group key, exact application envelope, and commitment list,
the factors are

$$
\rho_1=39,\qquad \rho_3=17,\qquad \rho_5=17.
$$

The resulting group commitment and challenge are

$$
R=4,\qquad c=5.
$$

The Lagrange coefficients at zero are

$$
\lambda_1=7,\qquad \lambda_3=9,\qquad \lambda_5=26.
$$

The three response shares are:

| $i$ | $z_i$ | $g^{z_i}$ | public right side | Accepted? |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|:---:|
| 1 | 3 | 64 | 64 | Yes |
| 3 | 27 | 25 | 25 | Yes |
| 5 | 38 | 48 | 48 | Yes |

Aggregation gives

$$
z=3+27+38=68\equiv27\pmod{41}.
$$

The final signature is

$$
(R,z)=(4,27).
$$

The ordinary verifier obtains

$$
g^{27}\equiv25\pmod{83}
$$

and

$$
R Y^c=4\cdot48^5\equiv25\pmod{83}.
$$

The signature therefore verifies.

### What the worked numbers do not prove

The group has only 41 scalars. Hash collisions, zero challenges, identity
aggregates, and discrete-log recovery are easy. The numbers prove that the code
implements the equations; they do not demonstrate cryptographic security.

## Common algebra mistakes

1. Using degree $t$ instead of $t-1$ for a $t$-share threshold.
2. Interpolating with list positions rather than explicit identifiers.
3. Using $i-j$ instead of $j-i$ without compensating for the sign.
4. Forgetting to reduce scalar operations modulo $q$.
5. Reducing group elements modulo $q$ instead of the group modulus $p$.
6. Calculating $R$ from a different commitment order at different signers.
7. Using $Y$ where the partial check requires $Y_i$.
8. Aggregating a subset of responses different from the commitment-list set.
9. Verifying the final signature over the raw payload when the application
   intentionally signed a bound envelope.

Every one of these boundaries has a corresponding Version 0.5 validation or
test.
